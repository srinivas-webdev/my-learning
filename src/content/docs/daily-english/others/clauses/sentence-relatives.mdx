        <div class="ml-10 leading-tight">
          <mark class="font-bold">Adjective Clauses</mark> <span class="underline underline-offset-2 decoration-4 decoration-red-500 mx-1">usually</span>
          <mark class="font-bold">modify a noun</mark> that they immediately follow.   
          The relative pronoun <mark class="bg-sky-300 font-bold italic">which</mark> is used to
          <mark class="bg-sky-300 font-bold">refer to things</mark> (not people).  

          **Adjective clauses that add descriptive information about a noun**
          but are **not essential** are punctuated with **commas**.
          - Boston, **which is in the state of Massachusetts**, is an interesting city to visit.
        </div>
      </li>

        <div class="flex items-center">

          <span class="text-body font-bold">What does the Research show?</span>
        </div> 
        <div class="ml-10 leading-tight">
          **Some adjective clauses** do not modify a noun. Instead they 
          <mark class="bg-lime-300 font-bold">comment on the whole idea in the preceding clause</mark>.  
          They are called <mark class="bg-lime-300 font-bold">"sentence relatives."</mark> They always use <mark class="font-bold">which</mark>.  
          - Well, I don't have time to do it for this project, 
          **which means it'll probably have to get put off until next year**.   
          
          **Sentence relatives** are **common** in <mark class="bg-lime-300 font-bold">conversation</mark>.
          They are also **common** in <mark class="bg-lime-300 font-bold">newspaper</mark> and <mark class="bg-lime-300 font-bold">fiction writing</mark>, 
          but they are rare in academic writing.
        </div>
      </li>
